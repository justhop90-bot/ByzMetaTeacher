There is a dangerous moment in any long AI project when the machine begins to look complicated enough that everyone involved starts mistaking complexity for destiny. The code has grown. The constants have multiplied. There are goals for this, strategic numbers for that, timers watching other timers, production rules feeding demands, demands waking capabilities, capabilities waiting on resources, resources being claimed and released, and somewhere in the middle of it all a poor Byzantine villager is standing beside a tree wondering why nobody has told him what to do. At that point the temptation is to declare the architecture itself the achievement. That would be a mistake. ByzMetaTeacher is not supposed to win because it has 858 rules. It is supposed to win because, when the game changes, it changes with it, and because every mechanism underneath that decision exists for a reason a good `.per` scripter would recognize.
The more deeply I compare the current controller with the old community scripts, the official evolution of the DE AI, and the actual habits of the serious custom-AI tradition, the clearer the answer becomes. The bot we want is not a copy of Naga, not a Byzantine Promisory, not a cleaned-up Stock AI, and not TheByzantineShadow wearing a military cloak. It should be something more specific: a community-native, competitive, adaptive Byzantine controller whose strategy is persistent, whose execution is disciplined, whose reactions are driven by observed game state, and whose style remains recognizably rooted in the practical `.per` culture that accumulated these techniques in the first place. The community did not arrive at its best scripts by discovering one perfect architecture. It arrived there through years of watching the engine misbehave, discovering which predicates could be trusted, learning which actions were merely requests, finding out which state had to persist, and then hammering those lessons into working rules. That is the tradition this bot should inherit.
The official evolution of the DE AI points in exactly the same direction. Over successive updates, the developers repeatedly improved strategy selection, adaptation to civilization strengths, adaptation to map type and enemy army composition, resistance to tower rushes, economic progression, timing of age advancement, willingness to choose fights, preservation of critical military upgrades, and retreat behavior. The scripting surface itself has also steadily expanded, including new strategic numbers, more goals, XS support, and increasingly detailed engine observations. That history matters because it tells us what "meta AI" actually means in this environment. It does not mean elegant software. It means increasingly better decisions expressed through the primitives the engine gives us. The best community style is therefore not architectural purity. It is accumulated engine literacy.
That gives us the first hard conclusion: ByzMetaTeacher should be a **competitive heuristic controller**, not an abstract planning engine. Its strategy layer should reason in the vocabulary that AoE2 actually supports: threat, posture, demand, target, production capacity, resource pressure, army floor, technology package, timing, and opportunity cost. Its lower layers should turn those decisions into ordinary `.per` actions. The strategy should say what must become true; capability logic should make the required infrastructure possible; feasibility should determine whether the attempt can happen now; the engine should receive the command; and the world state should be the witness. That is the central grammar. Everything else is implementation detail.
This also clarifies what should not become part of the bot's identity. The procedural machinery found in older competitive scripts is useful when it solves a real `.per` problem, but we should not import it merely because it is old. Naga's procedural jumps, Promisory's enormous rule libraries, Stock AI's sprawling generalized machinery, and Shadow's explicit transaction states are all products of particular problems and particular historical solutions. They are evidence, not scripture. The correct question is never "Does the famous script do this?" The correct question is "What engine behavior made this mechanism necessary, what strategic problem was it solving, and is that problem real here?" The answer determines whether the mechanism belongs. The community's instincts should be copied. Its fossil record should not.
The second hard conclusion is that the bot's strategy must live above individual actions. This is already one of the strongest things in the present design. The current controller does not simply react to an enemy archer by issuing a Skirmisher command. It observes threats, writes strategic state, derives standing targets, establishes production requirements, and lets the military layer consume those requirements. It maintains standing army floors independently from attack-group size, computes attack reserve above that floor, uses the engine's attack-group machinery rather than manually herding ten soldiers across the map, and has explicit retreat and attack-cycle recovery. The attack lifecycle itself is now recognizably community-native: establish authority, form the group through the engine, let `attack-now` do the work, observe whether the minimum package actually exists, reset the engine state, and only then begin another cycle. The current code even distinguishes the standing army from the attack group, which is exactly the distinction a real player makes instinctively and a sloppy bot forgets immediately. The `attack-soldier-count >= 10` witness, the reserve calculation, the retreat gate, and the use of `up-reset-attack-now` are all examples of the right kind of engine-specific discipline.
But this is where the audit has to become less flattering. The present attack doctrine is still more rigid than the community meta we actually want. Relative military parity is useful, but it is not the same thing as battlefield quality. Ten Spearmen do not equal ten upgraded Knights. Ten Crossbows do not equal ten Skirmishers. Ten units standing on paper do not equal ten units with the right upgrades, production replacement, siege support, positioning, or counter relationship. The current controller has improved this with counter-level vetoes, standing role targets, siege demand, and attack reserves, but the final bot should think in terms of **usable combat power**, not merely unit-count parity. We do not need a simulation, and we should not build one. We do need the next practical layer of community heuristics: composition quality, upgrade state, army-role completeness, and the strategic value of the intended fight. Otherwise "relative force >= 0" becomes a dangerously clean number hiding a filthy reality.
The same distinction appears in scouting. The current scouting layer is no longer decorative. It has a normal land-exploration group, a target-directed scouting path, threat observation, opening detection, and map-aware opening selection. That is a proper foundation. Yet a truly meta controller cannot treat scouting as merely "find the enemy and know whether he built a Stable." The official AI direction itself has repeatedly emphasized map awareness, adaptation to enemy composition, and responsiveness to changing circumstances. Scouting must therefore become decision-grade information, not merely target confirmation. The bot should scout to answer questions that alter decisions: Is the opponent actually committing to cavalry? Is this ranged pressure transitional or permanent? Is the opponent expanding? Is the army massing somewhere dangerous? Is the enemy technology timing consistent with the observed economy? Is the map itself forcing a different economic or military posture? The final controller does not need omniscience. It needs to know which uncertainties matter.
The opening system is another place where the present design is good but not yet the final creature. The current controller explicitly distinguishes Arabia, Arena, and generic maps; recognizes immediate, confirmed, and suspected pressure; stores an opening underlay; allows Anti-Rush to override the base plan; recognizes market investment without military infrastructure as a greed signal; and returns to the underlying plan once pressure disappears. That is already much closer to competitive community thinking than a fixed opening. But it also exposes the boundary of the current scope. The bot is currently reasoning about a deliberately narrow land-map world: Arabia, Arena, and everything else. That is fine if the project is explicitly a **1v1 standard land-map Byzantine meta controller**. It is not fine if we quietly start calling it a universal AoE2 AI. The current DE ecosystem is broader than that, and the 2026 game has continued to expand and change water play and map behavior. The final identity should therefore be honest about its battlefield. We should build the best Byzantine 1v1 land controller we can, not pretend we are building an empire-wide oracle.
That narrowing actually makes the project stronger. Current ladder data illustrates why. Recent 1v1 ranked data places Byzantines around a 46.6% win rate across all ratings with roughly a 2.2% pick rate over the last ninety days, with substantial matchup and map variation. Whatever one thinks of those aggregate statistics, the engineering implication is clear: a serious Byzantine controller cannot be built around one universal "best Byzantine strategy." The civilization needs flexibility because the position changes the value of its tools. That means the final bot should not chase a mythical permanent Byzantine build. It should express Byzantine **optionality** through persistent strategic demands and matchup-sensitive package selection.
This is where the Byzantine identity needs to become more precise. The bot should not be a Cataphract bot. It should not be an anti-cavalry bot. It should not be an Elite Skirmisher bot with a monastery attached. Byzantines are interesting because the civilization's strength lies in having a broad counter-oriented toolbox and the ability to make the opponent pay for choosing the wrong army. That makes the proper strategic personality one of **adaptive counterweight**. The bot should invest in the cheapest credible response early, preserve the ability to switch roles, and then become increasingly selective as the economy grows. Spears, Skirmishers, Crossbows, Camels, Cavalry, Monks, Siege, and eventually Cataphracts should not be treated as disconnected unit packages. They are instruments in a sequence of decisions about what the opponent is forcing the Byzantines to become. That is a much stronger identity than "Byzantines have good counters."
The research architecture is moving toward exactly this standard. The current controller has separated strategic admissibility from execution reliability. Package selection chooses whether a technology is strategically wanted; local providers determine whether it can execute; failed execution creates bounded cooldown rather than permanent death; research completion is observed from engine state rather than inferred from a retry counter; capability loss can invalidate local research claims; and shared resource control is protected against unrelated failures. That is not merely tidy. It is exactly the kind of reliability discipline that lets a heuristic controller behave like a player instead of a collection of frightened scripts. But the final research system must also respect opportunity cost. A strong player does not simply buy every useful technology once it becomes available. They ask what the next resource does to the current army, the next production cycle, the next age-up, and the next strategic window. Official AI development has explicitly emphasized preserving economy while advancing and being less eager to fight while critical army upgrades are still underway. Our final research doctrine therefore should not be "research correctly." It should be "research when the technology's strategic value exceeds what those resources would buy elsewhere."
The economy must obey the same principle. The current farm system has moved well beyond a fixed farm sequence. It uses population and food-gatherer pressure to establish demand bands, reserves, depletion thresholds, and mill scaling. The resource allocator has a single ownership section for the gatherer percentages, and the controller has enough strategic state to distinguish booming, pressure, military, and age-transition resource postures. That is good `.per` practice. It is practical, legible, and consistent with the old community style, where fixed percentages were often far more reliable than trying to calculate perfect continuous allocation. But the final economy should not become a spreadsheet in disguise. Its job is to answer a much simpler question: **what resource shortage is currently preventing the chosen strategy from functioning?** Food pressure should feed villager production and military. Wood pressure should feed farms, houses, ranges, stables, siege infrastructure, and Town Centers. Gold pressure should feed the chosen premium composition and technology package. Stone should appear when the strategic plan actually justifies it. Resource allocation should follow demand rather than existing simply to maintain attractive percentages.
There is another important lesson in the current code: the production-capacity work is heading in the right direction because it treats production buildings as consequences of persistent army demand rather than independent strategic decisions. The current Barracks, Range, and Stable rules scale production capacity from standing-force targets. That is much closer to mature community scripting than the old pattern of "we need this building because the strategy says so." The final bot should keep this. A player does not say "I enjoy owning two Barracks." They say "I need to replace this army at the rate the fight is consuming it." Production capacity is therefore a function of army requirement, not decoration. The same principle should govern siege workshops, Monasteries, and later production scaling.
This also tells us what the final bot must not become: a giant simulation of the economy. We do not need to calculate income per second for every resource, predict the exact future price of every research package, estimate combat outcomes to five decimal places, or construct a universal scheduler that moves invisible jobs through a queue. That would take us away from the community style and toward architecture theater. The old competitive scripts are powerful precisely because they reduce the game to decision-relevant signals: enough army, not enough army; enough farms, not enough farms; enemy has cavalry, enemy does not; Castle is feasible, Castle is not; pressure is real, pressure has disappeared; the current package is worth the gold, or it is not. The sophistication comes from choosing the signals, not from multiplying them until nobody remembers what they mean.
The most important thing, then, is that the final bot should have a recognizable **strategic personality**. It should feel like a Byzantine player who prefers to make the opponent's choice expensive. It should scout enough to know what question needs answering. It should choose an opening according to map and early evidence. It should maintain an army floor even when it is not attacking. It should turn surviving pressure into continued strategic value instead of resetting at each age boundary. It should create production capacity because its standing targets demand it. It should research because the army or economy gives the technology a reason to exist. It should boom when the battlefield permits it, not because a timer said the boom phase has arrived. It should retreat when the fight is bad rather than donating the entire army to history. It should return after the withdrawal once the strategic conditions recover. And it should be able to change composition without behaving as though the previous army was a sacred oath written in marble.
That is the real meaning of "meta" for this project. Not the largest code base. Not the most elaborate architecture. Not the most famous script name. It means the bot is making decisions that resemble the practical decisions a strong contemporary player makes, using the same rough evidence a player would use, expressed through the engine-native mechanisms the AoE2 scripting community has spent years proving useful. The community's old scripts teach us how to speak the language. The current DE AI teaches us what kinds of strategic adaptation matter. Byzantine design teaches us what to say with that language. Reliability engineering teaches us how to keep the sentence from collapsing halfway through.
So, is this the final bot we want to build? **Yes in identity, no in completion.** The identity should now be frozen. I would not keep redesigning the architecture every time we discover another interesting old AI. The target is clear: a **1v1 standard-land, contemporary Byzantine competitive heuristic controller**, written in a deliberately community-native `.per` style, centered on persistent strategic posture and demand, with practical capability ownership, bounded execution recovery, resource opportunity cost, production scaling, composition adaptation, and continuous reassessment. That is the bot. What remains is not to invent another architecture. What remains is to make every existing subsystem deserve that name.
And the standard for judging those remaining systems should be brutal. A strategic demand must have a real reason to exist. A capability must have a consumer. A consumer must have a meaningful condition. An execution rule must be authorized by actual feasibility. A command must have a world-state witness. Temporary state must eventually die. Persistent demand must survive temporary failure. Resource claims must release when their owner is finished, not when a timer happens to feel philosophical. Production capacity must follow actual army requirements. Research must compete with other uses of the same resources. Scouting must change decisions, not merely produce information nobody reads. Strategy must respond to the board, not to the order in which somebody happened to write the rules. And the game must remain the final judge, because the script can lie, the parser can smile, and the comments can insist that everything is correct while twelve villagers quietly gather stone for a technology the army will never use.
The finished ByzMetaTeacher should therefore feel less like a program executing a plan and more like a veteran player maintaining a position. It should have memory without becoming rigid, adaptability without becoming hysterical, specialization without becoming predictable, and reliability without becoming bureaucratic. It should know that a failed building attempt is an execution problem, that an obsolete army is a strategic problem, that a missing production building is a capability problem, that an empty queue is an operational problem, and that an enemy composition is an informational problem. Those are different problems. The final bot should treat them as different problems.
That is the distinction I would now consider non-negotiable. **The bot should not merely know what to build. It should know why the thing is needed, what has to exist before it can be built, what prevents it from being built, what evidence proves that it exists, what strategic purpose it serves, what resources it displaces, and what should happen when the battlefield changes its mind.**
That is not a new architecture. It is the final definition of the existing one.
The old community ghosts can stay in the walls. Naga can keep its scars. Promisory can keep its machinery. Stock AI can keep its ugly old tricks that somehow still work. Shadow can keep its forensic instruments and its suspicious little magnifying glass. We do not need to exorcise any of them. We need to know what each ghost knows.
Then ByzMetaTeacher can speak in its own voice.
Not the voice of Stock AI.
Not the voice of Naga.
Not the voice of Shadow.
The voice of a Byzantine player who has spent too long listening to all three.
And who, after all that noise, finally knows what matters.
**See the position. Decide what it means. Preserve the intent. Build what the intent requires. Spend with purpose. Execute only when the engine permits it. Believe the world, not the command. Recover without forgetting. Reassess without panicking. And make the opponent live with the consequences.**
That is the house style.
That is the standard.
That is the bot we should be building.
